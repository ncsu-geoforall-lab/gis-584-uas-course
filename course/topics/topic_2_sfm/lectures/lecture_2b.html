From flight to products

Section opener. The big picture first: what comes home from the field, what goes into the software, and what comes out. Every button in Metashape maps to one box on the next slide.

The photogrammetric pipeline

Images come in, features are matched, cameras are solved, dense geometry is built, cartographic products are exported. The dashed arrow is the part students forget: if alignment is weak you go back, clean images or add ground control, then re-optimize. Photogrammetry is iterative, not a single pass.

What comes home from the field

Three things: images, a flight log or EXIF positions, and ground control coordinates. Everything else is computed

The better these three inputs, the less cleanup later. The rest of this section takes them one at a time.

Digital imagery

  • Usually on the camera’s SD card
  • Often geotagged: the camera position is written into each photo’s EXIF header
  • Geotags are the position of the camera, not of anything in the picture, and are accurate to meters unless the aircraft has RTK or PPK
  • Look through them before processing: blurred, over-exposed, and takeoff frames go

Reminder from 2A: a geotag gets the model into the right neighborhood and seeds the alignment; centimeter accuracy comes from RTK/PPK positions or ground control. The pre-processing step nobody likes is culling bad frames, and it pays off more than any setting in the software.

Focal length and perspective

A short focal length close to the subject gives full perspective: objects near the camera look much larger, scale varies across the frame, distortion grows toward the edges. A longer focal length farther away approaches weak perspective, where rays are nearly parallel and scale is nearly uniform. UAS mapping sits between the two: wide action cameras push toward full perspective, higher flights with a longer lens toward weak perspective. Flight height and lens choice both matter for geometry, and overlap is still what makes the solution robust.

Flight log

  • The onboard IMU and GNSS receiver log position and attitude for every photo
  • Written to a text file, or straight into the photos’ EXIF (most DJI aircraft)
  • Contains the elements of exterior orientation
  • Describes the camera, not the ground: the model still needs tie points and control to fit the world

Flight attitude: the aircraft’s orientation in space, yaw, pitch, and roll

Logs seed the alignment and then bundle adjustment tightens them. When positions live in EXIF, Metashape reads them automatically; when they are in a separate log you import them with the right column order, time offset, and coordinate system, then check that the cameras land on top of your site.

GCP coordinates

  • Surveyed coordinates of targets placed before the flight, or of photo-identifiable points surveyed later
  • Anchor the model to a coordinate system and let you measure its accuracy
  • Record the coordinate reference system you surveyed in, every time

GCP placement and the GCP versus checkpoint distinction were in Lecture 2A; here the point is bookkeeping. A GCP file without its CRS is a set of numbers, not coordinates, and mixed CRS is the most common cause of a model that lands hundreds of meters from where it should.

Coordinate reference systems

Geographic (GCS) Projected (PCS)
Units degrees meters or feet
Example WGS 84 (EPSG:4326) NC State Plane, NAD83(2011): EPSG:6543 in feet, EPSG:3358 in meters
Use geotags, raw GNSS measurement, DEMs, GIS analysis

Vertical datum: GNSS heights are ellipsoidal; maps use orthometric heights above the geoid. In North Carolina the two differ by about 30 m

Geotags arrive in WGS 84 degrees; the products should leave in a projected system with meters, which for our work is NC State Plane. The vertical datum trips up more projects than the horizontal one: if the GCPs are orthometric and the cameras ellipsoidal, the block fits at the wrong height until Metashape is told which is which. Assignment 2B walks through setting both.

Products

Section transition. Four products come out of the pipeline; students met them in 1A and 2A, so this is a quick pass with the emphasis on what each one is good for.

What comes out of processing

Orthomosaic as the basemap, DSM or DTM as the analysis surface, point cloud as the raw 3D evidence, textured model for visualization, and the report as the record of how good all of it is.

Orthomosaic

  • Aerial imagery corrected for perspective and terrain so that scale is uniform
  • A raster with red, green, and blue bands, georeferenced like any other
  • Metashape calls it an orthomosaic; orthophoto is the generic term

Every pixel is in the right place for measurement, as long as the surface it was draped on is right. Errors in the DSM show up as smeared building edges and doubled features; errors in control show up as the whole map being shifted.

Digital surface model

  • DSM, digital surface model: the visible surface, including trees, buildings, and cars
  • DTM or DEM, digital terrain model: bare earth
  • SfM produces a DSM by default; a DTM needs ground classification and filtering

The distinction matters for every later topic: canopy height is DSM minus DTM, hydrology needs the DTM, and comparing an SfM DSM to a lidar DTM is a classic wrong conclusion. Metashape can classify ground points in the point cloud and build a DTM from those only.

Point cloud

  • A set of points with x, y, z, usually color, sometimes a class
  • Represents the external surface of everything the cameras saw
  • Vector or raster? Neither quite: “a point cloud is a badly behaved raster” (Dale Lutz)

Treat it as raw geometric evidence: classify it, thin it, grid it to a surface. Exported as LAS, LAZ, or COPC it moves into GRASS and other tools for the terrain topics.

Orientation and control

Section transition into the vocabulary Metashape uses in its dialogs and report: interior orientation, exterior orientation, markers.

What the software needs

Input Where it comes from Required?
Digital images with overlap The flight Yes
Exterior orientation (camera position and attitude) Flight log or EXIF as a first guess, refined by bundle adjustment Yes, at least approximately
Interior orientation (camera model) Self-calibration during alignment, or a calibration file Solved automatically
Ground control points Field survey For survey-grade products and for accuracy reporting
Elevation model Once a prerequisite; now a by-product of SfM No
Software implementing the collinearity equations Metashape, WebODM, and others Yes

Lecture 2A covered the concepts; this is the checklist form. The historical column is worth a sentence: classical orthorectification needed a DEM as input, and SfM turned that requirement into an output, which is the reason it needs so much overlap.

Exterior orientation

Six elements for every photo: the camera position (X, Y, Z) and its angular orientation:

Photogrammetry Aviation Axis Motion
omega Roll x, flight direction wing tilt side to side
phi Pitch y nose tilt forward and back
kappa Yaw z rotation about the vertical

Logs and EXIF provide first guesses for all six; bundle adjustment refines them until the tie points fit to a fraction of a pixel. In the Metashape reference pane these are the camera rows; their accuracy setting tells the adjustment how much to trust them.

Interior orientation

  • The camera model: focal length, principal point, and lens distortion coefficients
  • In the past: a laboratory calibration report for a metric camera
  • Now: self-calibration, estimated from the images during alignment

Self-calibration is what lets a consumer camera produce usable maps, and it is also a source of the doming error from 2A: with weak geometry the adjustment can trade focal length against a bowed surface and still fit. GCPs and a second flying height constrain it. The report prints the solved camera model with its uncertainties.

Ground control points in processing

  • A marker in Metashape is a GCP: known X, Y, Z plus its projections placed on the photos
  • Used to set the coordinate system, to optimize the alignment, and to measure error
  • Checkpoints are markers left unchecked in the reference pane: they are measured, not fitted

Marker placement is the manual part of the workflow and Assignment 2B walks through it: import the coordinates, filter photos by marker, drag the flag onto the target in several photos, optimize. The report then lists control error and checkpoint error separately; students should be able to say which one they would quote.

Processing in Metashape

Section transition. Options first, then the Metashape workflow step by step, with the settings that matter and what they cost.

Where to process

Option Examples Trade-off
Desktop, commercial Agisoft Metashape, Pix4Dmatic, DJI Terra Control and transparency; needs a capable machine and a license
Desktop, open source WebODM (Topic 7) Free, scriptable, same pipeline; fewer guardrails
Cloud DroneDeploy, Pix4Dcloud, WebODM Lightning, Drone2Map No local compute; recurring cost and less control over settings

The real questions: budget and equipment, how often you fly, whether you have the training to process, and whether you have the time

This course uses Metashape (licensed for every student) and WebODM (free). RealityScan handles aerial blocks too and is free for students, so it is a fair third option for personal projects. Cloud services trade money for time and hide the parameters we spend the rest of this lecture on.

Agisoft Metashape

  • Image-based reconstruction from still images, for aerial and close-range work alike
  • Alignment and reconstruction are automated; the operator chooses parameters and places control
  • Version 2.3.2, Professional edition, licensed for every student in this class
  • User manual, Python API, YouTube channel
  • Tutorials: orthomosaic and DEM with GCPs, point cloud classification and DTM, DEM-based measurements

Metashape 2.x renamed several steps: the dense cloud is now the point cloud, build mesh is build model, and the orthophoto is the orthomosaic. Older tutorials and videos use the old names; the pipeline is the same. Keep the manual open during the assignment; the appendix has the memory requirements per quality setting.

This is the map of the rest of the lecture and of Assignment 2B. Check the diagnostics after every step before spending compute on the next one: a bad alignment makes everything downstream wrong, and it is the cheapest step to redo. The model step is optional for mapping; the DEM can be built from the point cloud or straight from depth maps.

Loading photos and positions

  • Add Photos, then check the reference pane: positions from EXIF load automatically
  • Import a flight log when positions are not in EXIF: set the column order, coordinate system, and time offset
  • Set camera accuracy (meters and degrees) to what the aircraft’s GNSS can actually deliver

The picture to expect: cameras in neat flight lines above the footprint of the site. If they are scattered or in the ocean, the CRS or the column order is wrong. Camera accuracy is a weight, not a guess: 3 to 10 m for a standard GNSS, 0.02 to 0.05 m for RTK.

Align photos

  • Detects features in every image and matches them across overlapping images
  • Solves camera positions, orientations, and the camera model together (bundle adjustment)
  • Output: tie points (the sparse cloud) and camera poses
Accuracy Highest High Medium Low, Lowest
Images upscaled 2x full resolution downscaled 4x 16x, 64x
Use small, high-quality sets the usual choice large projects triage

Start at High; drop to Medium only for very large projects, and use Low for a quick check that the block hangs together before placing markers. Reference preselection uses the geotags to decide which pairs to match and saves a lot of time on aerial blocks. If coverage is thin, revisit image quality and overlap rather than the settings.

Bundle adjustment

  • Non-linear least squares over every camera and every tie point
  • Minimizes reprojection error: the distance between where a 3D point projects and where the feature was detected
  • Refines exterior and interior orientation at the same time
  • Markers with known coordinates strengthen and georeference the solution

The mathematical heart of the workflow, and the reason the report’s tie point statistics matter: a mean reprojection error above about one pixel says the camera model or the matches are poor. Optimize Cameras re-runs the adjustment after markers are placed; everything built before that (point cloud, DEM) must be rebuilt afterwards because the geometry moved.

Build point cloud

Quality Ultra high High Medium Low, Lowest
Image scale full, a point per pixel 1/2 1/4 1/8, 1/16
Cost days on a laptop hours; most real work under an hour; the class setting quick previews

Metashape computes a depth map for every image and fuses them into the point cloud. Quality is the setting with the biggest effect on time and memory; the manual’s appendix lists RAM per quality and image count. Medium is the assignment setting so that the lab finishes in class; High is what you would use for a real product.

Depth filtering and editing

Depth filtering Use when
Mild Complex geometry with many small foreground details you want to keep
Moderate In between
Aggressive Aerial mapping; removes most outliers. The Assignment 2B setting
Disabled Only for very clean, high-quality imagery
  • Edit the point cloud afterwards if needed: select and delete stray points, or classify ground points for a DTM
  • Filter the tie points by reprojection error, reconstruction uncertainty, and image count, then optimize again

Filtering prunes outliers that come from poor texture, noise, and badly focused images. Aggressive is the aerial default; Mild is for objects with fine structure such as vegetation or thin poles. Cleaning tie points before optimization is the cheapest accuracy gain in the whole workflow.

Build model

Setting Options
Surface type Height field: aerial and terrain, 2.5D, low memory. Arbitrary: closed objects, 3D, high memory
Source data Depth maps or point cloud; tie points for a quick preview
Face count High, Medium, Low, or custom

Optional for mapping: DEM and orthomosaic can be built without a model; build one for a textured 3D product

Height field for anything flown nadir over terrain; arbitrary for the 2A-style objects and for facades. Face count controls memory and export size; generate a detailed master and decimate copies for the web. The assignment still builds a model so that students see the textured product, but a production mapping workflow often skips it.

Editing the model

  • Close holes: fills gaps left by insufficient overlap; required before any volume calculation, otherwise the volume is meaningless
  • Decimate: replaces a dense mesh with a lighter one
  • Filter by connected component size and polygon size to drop floating fragments
  • Export to an external editor for heavy repairs and import the result

Holes are common in UAS data because weather cuts flights short. Close them before computing volumes; leave them alone otherwise, since interpolated geometry is invented geometry. Filtering by component size is the quickest way to remove floaters over water or sky.

Build texture

Mapping mode Use
Generic Any object; as uniform a texture as possible
Adaptive orthophoto Terrain with buildings: flat areas textured orthographically, vertical faces separately
Orthophoto Terrain only; most compact, weaker on vertical faces
Spherical, Single camera, Keep uv Special cases: ball-shaped objects, one photo, existing parameterization

Blending: Mosaic (default) picks the most nadir view per pixel; Average blends all views

For terrain, Adaptive orthophoto or Orthophoto; the rest are for objects. Texture size sets the atlas resolution; 4096 is enough for the assignment. Texture is purely visual and does not affect the DEM or orthomosaic.

Build DEM

  • Projection: set the output CRS here; the assignment uses NC State Plane, EPSG:3358
  • Source data: point cloud (all points for a DSM, ground class for a DTM), depth maps, or model
  • Interpolation: Enabled (default) fills gaps visible in at least one image; Disabled keeps only observed cells and is the honest choice for accuracy checks
  • Resolution: leave the default, which follows the ground sampling distance

Set the projection once here and the orthomosaic follows it. Point cloud as source with the ground class selected is how Metashape produces a DTM. Turn interpolation off when you want to know where the data really are, on when you need a continuous surface for analysis.

Build orthomosaic

  • Surface: DEM (the usual choice for mapping) or Model (true orthophoto with building facades handled)
  • Blending mode: Mosaic (default) divides the imagery into frequency bands and blends each; Average uses a weighted mean of all photos; Disabled takes the most nadir photo per pixel and shows seams
  • Refine seamlines and the ghosting filter help with moving objects and canopy
  • Same CRS as the DEM; the orthomosaic can only be built in the DEM’s projection

Mosaic is the right default; Average reduces noise on uniform surfaces but blurs edges; Disabled is a diagnostic to see where seams fall. Building on the model instead of the DEM gives a true orthophoto where building sides do not smear, at the cost of model quality driving the result.

Exports and quality control

Cloud-optimized formats (COG for rasters, COPC for point clouds) are what the later topics read directly from the web bucket. Camera positions and calibration can also be exported (XML, omega phi kappa text, Bundler) for reuse in other software. Save the project after every stage so a failed step can be rerun without recomputing everything before it.

The processing report

Always generate it. It contains:

  • Survey data: image count, flying altitude, ground resolution, coverage area, camera model
  • Camera calibration with residual plots
  • Camera locations and error estimates
  • Ground control point and checkpoint errors, listed separately
  • Overlap map, DEM and orthomosaic previews, processing parameters

Quote the checkpoint RMSE: control point error is circular, the adjustment was told to fit those points

Assignment 2B asks students to explain the report, and this slide is the reading guide. Overlap map: dark areas with fewer than 3 images are where the reconstruction is weak. Calibration residuals: a systematic pattern means the camera model is not fitting. Control versus checkpoint: if checkpoint error is much larger than control error, the model is over-fitting the control and the distribution or weighting needs work.

Optimizing with markers

  1. Import marker coordinates and set their coordinate system
  2. Place each marker on several photos (filter photos by marker)
  3. Set marker accuracy (0.005 to 0.05 m) and camera accuracy to realistic values
  4. Optimize Cameras: the adjustment now fits the control
  5. Rebuild the point cloud, DEM, and orthomosaic; the geometry has moved

Batch process: queue the steps with their parameters and let them run unattended; the same steps are available from the Python API for repeatable projects

The step students skip is the last one: after optimization every derived product is stale. Batch processing is how you avoid babysitting a multi-hour job, and the Python API is the same workflow as a script, which becomes useful when you process repeat flights of the same site in the change-detection topic.
